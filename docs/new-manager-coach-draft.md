## You Got Promoted. Nobody Trained You.

You were good at the work, so they made you the boss of it. That's how almost every
frontline promotion happens.

Then Saturday comes. Somebody calls off, somebody else is forty minutes behind, and
a person you had lunch with last week tells you they're not doing something. Nobody
covered this. You're improvising in front of a crew that's watching to see what you
do.

If that's where you are right now, the rest of this page is the part nobody sat you
down and explained.

## What almost every new supervisor gets wrong

You try to stay liked.

It doesn't feel like a decision. It feels like being reasonable. You let the late
arrival slide because they had a rough week. You do the task yourself instead of
asking twice. You soften the ask until it sounds like a favor, because you still
remember being on that side of it.

Every one of those is a small loan against your own authority, and the interest
comes due in about three weeks. The crew learns which rules are real and which ones
depend on your mood. Then when you finally do hold the line, it lands as a mood
swing instead of a standard, and now you're the boss who changed.

The other failure mode is the opposite and it's just as common. You overcorrect,
lead through pressure, and get compliance for a month before your best person quits.

Both come from the same place: nobody taught you the third option.

## What actually works

**Hold the standard, flex the warmth.**

The standard is what has to happen. It doesn't move because you like somebody or
because it's a bad day. How you say it moves constantly — different person,
different history, different day.

New supervisors get this backwards. They flex the standard to protect the
relationship, when the relationship is exactly what survives a clear standard and
what erodes under an unclear one.

**The standard is the kindness.** People can't hit a target they can't see. Leaving
somebody guessing about whether they're doing okay isn't generous, and they find out
what you actually thought during a review or a termination, which is the worst
possible time.

**Find out before you decide.** Somebody failing at a task might just not know how
to do it. Nobody may have ever shown them. That question costs you thirty seconds
and it changes what the conversation is.

## The one you're dreading: managing people you used to work beside

This is the hardest version and it's the most common one, because frontline
promotions come from inside.

Here's the situation. Somebody you're friendly with has been clocking in five to
seven minutes late most shifts. Not enough to write up. Enough that the crew has
noticed, and enough that two other people have started drifting later too.

Most new supervisors open one of two ways.

**The apology open:**

> "Hey, I hate to even bring this up, and honestly it's not a big deal, but I've
> been asked to say something about the clock-in times..."

You've already lost. You told them it isn't important, you blamed somebody above
you, and you left with nothing agreed.

**The hammer open:**

> "You've been late all week. This is your verbal warning. Fix it or we go to
> paperwork."

You'll get compliance and you'll spend it. That person stops bringing you problems,
and you won't notice what that cost until something breaks that you should have heard
about a week early.

**What works better:**

> "You've clocked in five or six minutes late four of the last five shifts. I need
> you here at your start time, ready to go. Is something making that hard?"

Three things happen in those two sentences. You named the specific observable fact,
so there's nothing to argue about. You stated the standard plainly, with no apology
and no threat. Then you asked, which gives them room to tell you their kid's daycare
opens at six and this is a scheduling problem rather than an attitude problem.

You don't need to be tough. You need to be specific, and then you need to shut up
and listen to the answer.

## How Frontline Coach helps

The gap for most new supervisors isn't knowing what they want. You already know the
person should show up on time. The gap is the words, in the moment, with that
specific person in front of you.

- **AI Coach** — describe what's happening on your shift and get a plan you can run
  today, not a framework to study.
- **Conversation Builder** — walk in with your opening line, your observable facts,
  and the one thing you need different. Three things, not a script.
- **Practice** — rehearse it against an AI employee who pushes back, deflects, and
  brings up something you didn't plan for. Better there than in the break room.
- **Pushback Coach** — the words when somebody argues while you're standing right
  there.
- **Skill vs. Will Diagnostic** — work out whether it's a skill problem, a will
  problem, or yours.
- **Documentation Assistant** — turn rough notes into a clean, factual record.

Free to start. Built by a Navy veteran and eighteen-year frontline operator who
[learned all of this the wrong way first](/operator).

## What it won't do

It isn't HR and it isn't a lawyer. It doesn't know your company's policies, your
union contract, or your state's employment law, and it will never tell you to fire
somebody or discipline anybody. It organizes your thinking and gives you language.
Every decision stays yours, and your company's process still governs.

It also won't make you a leader in a week. Doing the boring part repeatedly is what
does that. This just means the boring part doesn't have to start with you guessing.

## Questions new supervisors actually ask

**I just got promoted and I have no idea what I'm doing. Is that normal?**

Yes, and it's the standard experience rather than a sign you weren't ready. Most
frontline promotions come with a new badge and no training. The people who get good
at it aren't more natural, they just get reps and feedback sooner.

**How do I manage people who used to be my coworkers?**

Change the standard first, not the friendship. Be specific and consistent about what
has to happen, and don't pretend the promotion didn't happen. The awkward stretch is
usually two or three weeks. Being unclear to avoid that stretch makes it last months.

**What if somebody doesn't respect me because I'm new or younger?**

Respect follows predictability more than it follows tenure. If you say what you
expect, hold it the same way for everybody, and follow through when you say you
will, that resolves faster than any speech about authority will.

**Should I write somebody up or just talk to them first?**

Talk first, and find out whether they actually know how to do the thing. Plenty of
performance problems are training problems wearing a costume. Follow your company's
progressive discipline process for anything formal — that process, not this page,
is what governs.

**How do I have a hard conversation without it turning into an argument?**

Lead with an observable fact instead of a characterization. "You clocked in six
minutes late four times this week" is hard to argue with. "You have an attitude
problem" is an opinion, and people defend themselves against opinions.

**Is there training for shift leads and first-time supervisors?**

Very little that's built for the frontline. Most leadership material is written for
executives and directors, who have training budgets and time to read. That gap is
why this app exists.
